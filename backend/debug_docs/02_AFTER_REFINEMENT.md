## Content

### 1. Executive Summary
The mini‑TOGAF audit examined the Interoperability Architecture Solutions against the European Interoperability Reference Architecture (EIRA) and the TOGAF Architecture Development Method (ADM). The review confirms strong alignment with EIRA’s standardized building blocks and governance framework, but highlights missing artefacts and formal processes in several ADM phases. While the architecture benefits from a clear vision and institutional support, detailed documentation, migration planning, and KPI definition are required to achieve full TOGAF compliance and reduce operational risk.

### 2. Architecture Vision & Scope
The architecture seeks to deliver seamless digital public services for EU administrations, businesses, and citizens, focusing on data exchange, service integration, and semantic consistency. Its scope is tightly linked to EU programmes such as the ISA programme, the Digital Europe Programme, and the European Interoperability Framework (EIF). Stakeholder engagement is evident, yet measurable business objectives and key performance indicators remain insufficiently defined.

### 3. Alignment with TOGAF ADM Phases
The current state of EIRA was mapped to each ADM phase:

- **Preliminary Phase** – Governance is provided through the Digital Europe Programme, but a formal Enterprise Architecture (EA) board charter is absent.  
- **Phase A – Architecture Vision** – The vision “building bridges for digital public services” is clear; explicit KPIs are missing.  
- **Phase B – Business Architecture** – Open‑data ecosystem focus is evident, yet detailed business process models and value‑stream maps are not documented.  
- **Phase C – Information Systems Architecture** – Architecture Building Blocks (ABBs) align with the EIF and include semantic models; a catalogue of application components and data‑flow diagrams would improve traceability.  
- **Phase D – Technology Architecture** – Recent releases (e.g., EIRA v6.1.0) introduce updates, but a concrete technology‑stack mapping to standards (cloud platforms, APIs) is lacking.  
- **Phase E – Opportunities & Solutions** – Iterative improvements are noted, but a formal solution roadmap with milestones is absent.  
- **Phase F – Migration Planning** – No migration plan exists; transition architectures, phased migrations, and resource estimates need definition.  
- **Phase G – Implementation Governance** – Community collaboration mechanisms are informal; a structured implementation governance framework is required.  
- **Phase H – Architecture Change Management** – Ongoing version updates suggest change management, yet a formal change‑request and impact‑analysis process is missing.  
- **Requirements Management** – Stakeholder benefits are listed, but a requirements‑traceability matrix is absent.

### 4. Strengths
The architecture leverages standardized Building Blocks that provide a reusable, EIF‑compliant foundation, and semantic consistency that enhances cross‑border data discoverability. Governance is anchored in the Digital Europe Programme, delivering strong institutional backing, while regular releases (e.g., v6.1.0) demonstrate a commitment to continuous improvement.

### 5. Gaps & Risks
Key gaps include the absence of Business Architecture artefacts (process models, value‑stream maps), an undefined migration roadmap, incomplete technology‑stack mapping, informal change‑management practices, and limited KPI definition. These gaps pose risks of inefficiency, budget overruns, integration challenges, architectural drift, and difficulty measuring interoperability success.

### 6. Recommendations
- **Formalise Architecture Governance** – Draft an EA charter, define roles (EA Lead, Architecture Review Board), and establish decision‑making procedures.  
- **Develop Detailed Architecture Artefacts** – Produce Business Architecture models (process maps, stakeholder maps, capability models), an Information Systems catalogue (application inventory, data‑flow diagrams, interface specifications), and a Technology Architecture matrix linking ABBs to concrete standards and security controls.  
- **Create a Migration & Implementation Roadmap** – Outline short‑, medium‑, and long‑term milestones, align releases with EU funding cycles, and allocate resources for each migration phase.  
- **Implement Requirements Management Discipline** – Capture functional and non‑functional requirements in a traceability matrix linked to ABBs and stakeholder needs.  
- **Introduce a KPI Framework** – Define metrics such as the percentage of public services complying with EIRA, average onboarding time for new data sources, and quarterly cross‑border service interactions.  
- **Strengthen Community Engagement** – Leverage the Interoperable Europe Portal, social media, and collaboration channels to gather feedback, disseminate best practices, and drive adoption.

### 7. Conclusion
The EIRA initiative already embodies many TOGAF best practices, particularly in standardized building blocks and governance support. By addressing the identified gaps—especially detailed architecture documentation, migration planning, and formal change management—the architecture can achieve full TOGAF compliance, enhance interoperability outcomes, and deliver measurable value to EU public services.
