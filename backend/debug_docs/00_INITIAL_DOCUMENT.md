## **5.2 Application Architecture**
The Application Communication Diagrams, as presented, exhibit a few structural weaknesses that could undermine the resilience and scalability of the solution. First, the diagrams reveal a **single point of failure** in the central authentication service, which is directly referenced by multiple downstream components (e.g., the policy engine, data‑analytics module, and citizen‑portal interface). Because all request flows converge on that one service, any outage—whether due to hardware fault, software bug, or a denial‑of‑service attack—would immediately cascade across the entire ecosystem, rendering the public‑facing portals and internal analytics unusable. Mitigating this risk typically involves introducing redundant authentication instances behind a load‑balancer, and ensuring state replication (e.g., token caches) so that fail‑over can occur without loss of session continuity.

A second concern is the presence of **circular dependencies** between the “Regulation & Data‑Protection” component and the “Traceability & Transparency” service. The diagram shows the Regulation component invoking the Traceability service to verify compliance, while the Traceability service, in turn, queries the Regulation component for rule definitions before completing its audit. This bidirectional call pattern can lead to deadlocks or indefinite retry loops, especially under high load, and complicates versioning because any change in one service may force coordinated updates in the other. Refactoring the interaction into a publish/subscribe model—where the Regulation component publishes rule updates to a shared knowledge base and the Traceability service subscribes to those updates—breaks the cycle and simplifies deployment pipelines.

Finally, the overall **modularity** of the application components appears only loosely aligned with the Business Architecture articulated in Phase B. Phase B emphasizes a clear separation between citizen‑centric services, regulatory oversight, and analytical insight generation. In the current diagrams, the “Training & Knowledge Exchange” module is tightly coupled to the “Obstacles‑Management” component, sharing the same data store and synchronous API calls. This violates the intended modular boundary that should keep capacity‑building activities independent of operational bottleneck tracking. Re‑organising these functions into distinct bounded contexts—each with its own dedicated data schema and asynchronous communication via events—would honor the business‑level decomposition and improve both maintainability and agility as the AI governance framework evolves.
